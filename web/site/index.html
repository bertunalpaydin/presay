About Presay

A pre-flight check for what you are about to say, made for people who speak English as another language.

What it does

Paste the answer or talk you have prepared. Presay marks the words that listeners are most likely to mishear, shows how they are pronounced, explains what makes them tricky, and suggests easier words you could use instead. Then you can practise the marked words, read your whole script aloud, or pick one of the practice texts from everyday situations, and see what was heard.

How the marks work

  • Blue marks are words the speech model heard differently when you practised. Your own results always win over the general rules.
  • Red and amber marks come from sound and spelling patterns that are hard for speakers of your first language: for example the two “th” sounds, /w/ and /v/, /r/ and /l/, short vowels, consonant clusters, endings that get dropped or lose their voice, silent letters and long words with tricky stress. A word scores higher when a typical slip turns it into a different real word, like “west” into “vest” or “right” into “light”.
  • Your first language decides which of these count and how much. There are rule sets for speakers of ten languages. If yours is not listed, choose the one whose sounds are closest.
  • Explanations of why a word is hard and how to say it can be shown in English or in your first language. Everything else on the site stays in English.
  • Easier alternatives come from WordNet, a dictionary of synonyms. Presay only suggests common words that score clearly lower. Always check that the meaning still fits your sentence.

Privacy

Your script and your recordings never leave your device. The speech model (OpenAI’s Whisper) runs inside your browser. The first time you practise, your browser downloads the model files from Hugging Face and the speech software from the jsDelivr network, and then keeps them. Your word list and settings are saved in this browser only. Presay has no accounts, no tracking and no cookies.

Limits

  • A speech model is a stand-in for a human listener, not a perfect one. It sometimes understands a word from context even when it was said unclearly, and sometimes mishears a clear word. Practise a word a few times before trusting the result, and use “Ignore this recording” for bad takes.
  • Pronunciations come from an American English dictionary.
  • Each rule set is a summary of common tendencies. Speakers differ by region, by the other languages they know and by how they learned English, so your own practice results always count for more.
  • The explanations in other languages are new. If something reads wrong in your language, please say so on the project’s GitHub page.
